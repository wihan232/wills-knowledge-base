## Fallacy of Appealing to Authority

Also named the _ad verecundiam fallacy_, [[content/appeal-to-authority-04/index|Appeal to Authority]] refers to situations where evidence is accepted from an individual who is taken to be an authority but isn't a _real_ authority or _relevant_ authority. This person can happen to be someone who misrepresents themselves as an expert in a field in which they don't have special competence in. 

Generally, [[modern-examples-authority|modern examples]] of this fallacy can happen where a celebrity or famous figure sponsors or endorses a particular product, brand, or social movement. They would often use their platforms, taking advantage of their ability to reach wider audiences.

Later discussed in the [[history-of-authority|history]] section of this topic, Locke presented the name _ad verecundiam_ to literally mean, argument towards _modesty_. While at first glance, the term may sound ironic, as the arguer is obviously doing anything but being modest. Rather, Locke defined it to mean that the listener's assumption of the arguer is being modest was being exploited and taken advantage of. In essence, appeal to authority is a breach of modesty, where the logical shortcut is used to lead other to a conclusion because of their sense of modesty.
